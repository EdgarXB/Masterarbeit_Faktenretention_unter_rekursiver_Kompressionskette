The root of this divergence lies in cultural practices and wage structures. In the U.S., tips frequently supplement low base wages for workers like baristas, hotel staff, and cab drivers, making tipping a practical necessity for service providers. Abroad, however, service industry employees often receive living wages, reducing the need for tips. For instance, taxi drivers in Chile or New Zealand do not expect tips, and refusing to tip would not incur backlash.  

Hotels present the most complex tipping scenarios, as travelers interact with multiple staff members. Doormen who open cabs, bellboys who carry bags, and porters who deliver luggage to rooms all offer services, but etiquette dictates tipping only the last person in the chain—the individual who physically brings bags to the room. At checkout, tip only the staff member who loads luggage onto a cart and escorts it out, not those who merely assist in the process.  

Restaurants require careful attention to service charges. In contrast, restaurants in Fiji, Malaysia, and South Korea generally do not expect tips at all.

Concierges deserve tips only for exceptional help—such as securing hard-to-get theater tickets or providing special assistance—with $10–$20 (in local currency) being appropriate. Guides, whether for skiing in Switzerland, golf in Scotland, or fishing in Ireland, typically earn 10–15% tips, aligning with U.S. standards for their expertise.  

Practical tips can ease tipping stress: Obtain small bills or coins from the hotel front desk to avoid awkwardness with large notes; if only large bills are available, politely ask a porter or skycap for change—they are accustomed to this and not embarrassed.
