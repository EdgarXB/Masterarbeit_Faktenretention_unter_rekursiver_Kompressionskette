Starting with Pembroke, Illinois, Koner contacted an outreach worker who identified food as the critical need; she enlisted neighbors and childcare parents to send monthly boxes of food and household items, providing nearly 800,000 meals nationwide. To protect privacy, sponsor and recipient communities are kept distant—e.g., Atlanta chapters aid Kentucky families, or Florida families assist Navajo communities in New Mexico. Beyond material support, families exchange letters, photos, and stories about cultural traditions (e.g., Navajo life without electricity or running water), creating a "shared learning experience" Koner believes fosters broader world understanding.  

The economic downturn expanded outreach to Bedford-Stuyvesant (Brooklyn’s only urban site), where sponsors use PayPal and a local pantry distributes food. Koner also addressed unexpected need in her own middle-to upper-middle-class Hastings community, using alphabet-labeled donations for 11 families to safeguard privacy (some children attended the same schools).  

Despite challenges, Family-to-Family has seen growing enthusiasm, with new chapters launching instead of participants dropping out. Koner attributes this to heightened awareness of tough times, noting even struggling communities prioritize giving: "We’re all affected" by economic uncertainty, she argues, "we need to be there for each other and share."
